Wow, it's been two weeks since my last post. That's my bad. I was moving and super busy, so it feels good to take the time to sit down and write something out again. 

Recently I've seen some interesting internet chatter about the concept of "the veil". Specifically, places where the veil is thin, and the veil is thick. But as it turns out, my idea of what the veil is supposed to represent was completely wrong. Basically I made myself look like a stupid idiot when I told the majority of the readers of this blog yesterday about what the veil was. 

## What is the veil?

The veil is the curtain or division between worlds. In Paganism, this represents the connection the physical world has to the spiritual one. So for example, a cemetery or a house haunted by a ghost would have a thin veil, while H&M would have a thick veil. Or something.

People have called some notable locations places where the veil is thin. Like New Orleans, or the Grand Canyon. People have called other locations places where the veil is thick. Like Boston or Arlington, Virginia. Each to their own, I guess. 

But I can't believe I'm so dumb to the point I got my understanding of what the veil was wrong. Although maybe I should have used some context clues and really figured out what the hell people were talking about.

## My (incorrect) understanding of the veil

My veil is a little less metaphysical and existential and a little more pedestrian. And it also happens to be the concept of the veil we're talking about today. 

So my understanding of the veil was the separation from things the public is supposed to see versus things they're not normally supposed to see. The textbook example of this is in a restaurant. You don't normally see the food being made or brought to your table, so the veil is pretty thick. But if there's an open window to the kitchen and you can see the cooks, the veil is thinner. 

Other notable examples exist. The veil is really thin if you walk onto a plane using stairs or a ramp instead of a jetbridge. The veil is super thick from a stadium suite in a nice arena versus a courtside seat where you walk past service entrances and locker room doors to get to your...folding chair. (I'll never understand why basketball does this.)

Maybe a better explanation is how far away from the creation of a product, or the structure of it, you sit. For example, if you live in a house, you can climb up to your attic and see the structure and frame and insulation and wiring and plumbing. An apartment has no attic. You can't peek *through* the veil and see what's really inside your home. Unless something's gone horribly wrong, of course - which DOES happen.

Same goes for something I called scary a few blogs ago - computer errors. And this is an interesting case, because the veil on this keeps getting thicker and thicker over time. In the 90s and 2000s, you got these cryptic, scary walls of text that would give you some honestly pretty detailed information about what went wrong with your computer. Was it always helpful? No, not to the ordinary user. But it reminded you that you were only a couple layers and some bad luck away from your computer turning into a paperweight. Now all errors are just sort of, "We encountered a problem. Restart the application." Not helpful.

So in most cases the veil seems to be thick. People like to be separated from the detailed stuff these days and only really care about the end result. I don't care that my Red Bull has 47 artificial flavors in it! It tastes like a sudachi (whatever that is). I don't care that my chicken nuggets are ground up paste if they're shaped like dinosaurs. Which brings me to my final point...

## Are "thick veils" luxurious or just in style?

In the highest-tier airline statuses out there, you can be dropped off at a separate airport entrance, be whisked through private security and passport control, wait at a private lounge, and be the first person escorted to your gate through some secret door  in the airport. That is truly the thickest-veil experience. But there's no sense of adventure to it.

But is that really worth it over flying private? You walk out onto a tiny airport apron with a tiny building and onto the sun-baked tarmac onto a tiny plane, where your flight crew is all of two people. Internet if you're lucky. Your plane weighs less than some box trucks and gets thrown around in the sky on departure. Not as glamorous as it seems, but an adventure. 

You can go to a Michelin-star restaurant and be served caviar that's been sourced from a fish only fed Erewhon strawberries and given snail slime skincare every day. But you can also visit a roadside food truck and order a burger. One has more character than the other.

You can buy a $199 piece of software to automatically file your taxes. Or you can do them yourself and feel accomplished for doing something nobody would dare to do, knowing that you know the process front to back.

It kind of goes along with the idea of first principles. Obviously nobody's going to go back and build a couch starting from rocks and trees, but maybe there's something still fun and wonderful about doing things yourself. Going down the road less traveled. Doing something unglamorous and without luxury, but still a core memory to be made indeed.
