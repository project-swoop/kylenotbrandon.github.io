Studies have shown that recreational runners live about three years longer than their non-runner counterparts. This statistic shouldn't surprise you too much. However, only 15 percent of the US population engages in recreational running as an activity.

Over the last year and a half or so, I've taken it upon myself to rejoin this 15 percent of the US population. I've always been sporadic about it, but after I graduated from college I've begun to realize that life is short and life is too short to neglect your health. As of very recently I've been getting back in the gym as well. So basically I'm trying to atone for the last four years of whatever college dining hall food and cheap beer does to the body. 

But what I've noticed is that running REALLY sucks. It's not an enjoyable experience at any point. Obviously with it being July, 100-degree weather doesn't help that case but running on its own is interesting in the way that it would usually be incorporated into an enjoyable sport (e.g., soccer) but we choose to eschew the fun part and distill it down to just  the toil. Which does make sense, I guess.

## The history of running (that I didn't bother to do research on)

Humans are built to run (allegedly). We're adapted to run for miles and hours to outrun our prey and tire them out with endurance adaptations such as sweating, springed (?) feet and ankles, and breath control. And as the spirit of exceptionalism would have it, we've celebrated feats of endurance and speed in humans for millennia. Ever heard the story of the first marathon?

> *"The first person to ever run a marathon died, and that's why I don't run." - Former coworker* 

So we ran for survival, and then became agrarian and really only ran for sport, and then everyone kind of...stopped. Until the 1960s and 70s, of course, where running exploded in popularity again. Running is accessible, one of the most effective ways to build cardiovascular health, and releases endorphins. It's pretty much a form of meditation in itself. Actually, I got the inspiration to start this blog and this site while I was on a run. And, as we'll discuss later on, it's gotten people out of the house and around each other through social running clubs or gamified social platforms like Strava.

## My experience with running

Now I'm not an elite athlete...yet.

Just kidding, I know who I am. I'll likely never run a 5-minute mile, or a marathon, and that's alright with me. Because I used to stigmatize myself, and I was worried about how I would appear running around others. If there's one thing I'm not super keen on, it's being perceived in certain situations. Especially those where I can start overthinking and convincing myself, "Damn, those people are judging me." And because of that overthinking and mindset, I was always a little unwilling to go for a run sometimes. 

Honestly, living in a city where everyone runs and bikes has made me more comfortable doing it myself. You see people of all ages, shapes, and sizes doing their best. And that's really what running is about to me: self-improvement. To me, I shouldn't be doing it for anyone else but myself, and when I got myself into that mindset I started running (and working out in the gym) more regularly, too. I'm still not an incredibly confident person, but it's helped me a good bit. 

> *"Courage isn't doing everything without any reservations. That's called stupidity. It's weighing doing something and realizing that it's hard and trying it anyway."  - Some random lady I sat next to on a plane*

That being said, I still HATE running. I hate doing it. I hate the first mile. I hate the last mile. I hate the hydration beforehand. I hate the fact I can't do it for like 3 hours after I've eaten or I'll throw up. 

And more than anything, I hate myself during it. 

"Why aren't you better at this? Why aren't you faster? Why are you thinking about quitting?"

There's always that bit of the subconscious trying to tell you to stop. That it's too hard. That it's uncomfortable. And that tension between that part of me and my conscious saying, "You can do this, you've done this before, and by God you WILL finish it out" evokes some sort of emotion most adjacent to rage. And it's the source of my motivation DURING the run. Afterwards, it might be the fact that I'm happy I took a step to improve myself, or my pace, or my distance. But from start to stop, I'm generally pretty unhappy, and I do engage in a bit of what people would probably call self-loathing during the affair. 

I SWEAR this is a net positive experience for me. As I said, I've come to love it. But that doesn't mean I don't love ALL of it.

## Run clubs

As you may well know, I live in the greater DC area. I live in an area with good running trails. And as people tend to do here, they run pretty prolifically. But what people do that really ticks me off is join run clubs. 

As a bit of backstory, run clubs were popularized by Millennials as a response to the loneliness/social media epidemic. The idea's pretty simple: meet up somewhere, hang out and chat beforehand, go for a group run, and then go hang out somewhere else (like a cafe or a bar) afterwards. Pretty straightforward, and on paper a good idea for those looking for motivation or more social connection. 

And I should preface this by saying not all run clubs are created equal, and that there are probably some I would have fun in. But man oh man the ones here get on my nerves. 

All the running paths/sidewalks here are only 3 or 4 people wide. And as I alluded to earlier, I prefer running alone because I hate being perceived. But I've been running in one direction and a run club has been running the other way on multiple occasions, and these throngs of sweaty nerds in tank tops and short shorts WILL. NOT. MOVE. OVER. FOR. YOU.

I've been forced onto the grass. I've run into people before. I have every right to the sidewalk just as they do. So why don't they move over? Is it an ego thing? Is it a spatial awareness thing? Probably more likely the second than the first, but neither is a great excuse. 

I (and DC as a whole) am not the only one nothing this. This [article from Curbed](https://www.curbed.com/article/run-clubs-hatred-new-york-central-park.html) notes the same things that I do. Notably, a man by the name of Steve in the article says that he thinks run clubs are the antithesis of what running should be - a solo activity. I do see that as a personal preference thing, but it's interesting to see that he said it's pretty much against the ethos of the sport. (???)

But also as mentioned in the article, the behavior doesn't stop at bumping into people and being generally unaware. There's also just plain bad behavior. Littering, playing Bluetooth speakers in public, and - perhaps most offensive to some - taking over an establishment post-run.

See, I couldn't do that. I am well aware that when I run, I sweat. When I sweat, I smell bad. And when I smell bad, I want to shower. 

But these throngs of people come in after their run and mob an, in many cases, already-sweaty bar. And then, congratulations, you've ruined everyone else's experience.

In summary, and in all actuality, I do sympathize with people who join run clubs to an extent. These are people looking to hang out and be social in a city and world that can be antisocial at times, especially if your preferred pastime ISN'T drinking. But I like to be alone with my thoughts in my head and start angry and finish calmly. I fear running with others and holding conversation while running is:

1. Too hard of a task for me to successfully multitask and 

2. Doesn't allow me to be alone with my thoughts.

And I'm glad in the process of researching for this post and seeking other internet opinions I find that I'm not alone in this position. And once again - I'm not right in this. Nobody is ever right at everything. But at the same time, I can bet you far more people are annoyed at you run club people than at regular runners. So take that as you will.

So on your next run, stick to the right. Leave the Bluetooth speakers at home. And, if you can help it, shower before you do anything else.
